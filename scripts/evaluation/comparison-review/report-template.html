The target is a rolling survey average

The ladder series is a trailing three-year survey average: “2025” averages 2023–2025. The first two horizons overlap survey years included in the origin, so a very stable short-horizon forecast benefits from the target’s construction. This is not evidence that an annual wellbeing process was forecast precisely.

The existing source spans 2011–2025. A 2006-origin ladder experiment needs different source data and enough pre-origin history. Reusing already-seen later outcomes under a new origin is a useful robustness check, not a fresh independent test. Source definition and coverage.

Income, health, and policy effects remain different questions

The health entry wins pooled GDP error, but its GDP forecast is computed entirely from its GDP trend and fixed damping. Health does not enter that GDP calculation. Its wellbeing health coefficient is negative (−0.0137 ladder points per life-expectancy year), fitted from a short panel. Neither result tells us that improving health lowers happiness or that a health policy has been evaluated.

In #24, GDP is 64.3% worse than persistence in 2020, despite its strong 2025 result. A seven-year average hides crisis-year failures. All experiments here turn off AI displacement and transfer policy channels, so forecasting gains do not establish whether a bill, tax, or dividend improves wellbeing.

What I agree with—and what needs correcting

I reconstructed all eight saved model score sets from their point predictions, matching their MAE, root-mean-square error, bias, and comparator to within 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁴. I also independently reconstructed Claude’s decay fit and 1,400 forecast rows. The reported numbers are reproducible. The interpretations need these corrections.

1. The “registered metric” winner is wrong
Bad — the report crowns the pooled winner as the registered-primary winner. Use #24 for the 2025 primary ranking; name Claude’s separate pooled win.

Claude’s report says Winner on the registered metric: Claude country offset (0.2716, +6.3% skill). Both leading protocols explicitly register the 2025 wellbeing endpoint. #24 scores 0.3513 there; Claude’s fitted variant scores 0.3800. On pooled error, Claude wins 0.271606 versus 0.272122.

Comparison claim, line 39; Claude primary metric, line 33; #24 primary metric, line 343.

2. The proposed pair is not the best combination
Bad — the displayed five blends do not justify “better than any 3+ blend.” The full finite search finds counterexamples.

The report says Better than either alone and better than any 3+ blend. Its 50/50 offset pair gives 0.264465 pooled error, but equal thirds of country offsets, damped trends, and the original model give 0.262393. The best recipe in the declared catalog is 75% country offsets + 25% original, at 0.260920 pooled error. At the registered endpoint, the proposed 50/50 pair is slightly worse than #24 alone.

A weak standalone model can supply useful error cancellation. That does not rehabilitate its economic assumptions or make the selected combination a validated forecast. The winners were selected on these outcomes. Claim, line 39.

3. The health feature has not earned causal or incremental credit
Bad — beating persistence does not isolate whether health helped. Run a matched feature-removal comparison inside training data.

The health entry changes the regression, income extrapolation, and damping together. Its result does not separate the extra health variable from those other choices. No matched otherwise-identical no-health forecast is registered here. Its strong GDP score comes from a GDP-only extrapolation; calling that evidence for a health mechanism would be wrong.

Minimal fix: describe it as a promising forecast specification. Compare the same model with and without health using identical training splits, forecast covariates, and hyperparameter-selection budgets before an untouched evaluation. Negative health coefficient, lines 12–23; fit and forecast implementation.

4. Re-registration cannot make known outcomes fresh
Bad — a new origin or a new author does not supply an independent confirmation on the same known outcomes.

Claude properly labels its blend post-hoc, then proposes a fresh 2006-origin test. The current ladder panel cannot supply that origin; obtaining different historical data is a new research task. Even with those data, the 2019–2025 outcomes and the period’s broad events are already known. That experiment must remain retrospectively selected unless an actually untouched evaluation is protected.

The 1980 objective experiment has also already been scored, and its outcomes use different units. It cannot confirm a ladder blend. Separate authors checking one partition provide an implementation replication, not an independent data test. Fresh-holdout proposal; Claude’s own shared-partition caveat.

5. “Exactly one run” and the inner validation are not verified
Not verified — registration precedes the result in git, but the runner cannot prove one execution or no prior access.

The protocol commit cfe408e precedes the result commit cca606e. The checked-in fit uses only training inputs. However, run.ts reads test values before fitting, overwrites output files, and has no exclusive receipt or rejection of a repeated run. I found no committed reproducible output for the inner-validation numbers in the research note. This is a traceability gap, not evidence that the author cheated.

Minimal fix for future work: isolate fit/predict from scoring, freeze and hash code plus forecasts, then have a separately controlled scorer write an append-only receipt. Publish inner-fold results and all registered variants. overwrite/read order, lines 11–20; inner-validation claims, lines 27–32.

6. Small documentation errors obscure what was actually fitted
Correct the descriptions; these errors do not change the verified saved scores.

The lag counts are 298, 199, and 100, not 293, 196, and 99. The decay is a pair-count-weighted geometric mean of three annualized lag regressions, not a single joint nonlinear least-squares optimum. The “no lag dominates by count” code comment conflicts with that weighting. Claude’s GDP values differ from the original by at most 5.24 × 10⁻¹⁰ dollars: numerically equivalent, not byte-identical.

Also, setting decay to zero moves immediately to the anchor; it is not the original model’s gradual monthly relaxation. The note’s “published form” label for that inner comparison is wrong. And raw “number of fitted quantities” counts across the entries omit different numbers of country slopes, inherited coefficients, and tuning choices; they are not a comparable complexity score.

pair counts; estimator and comment; zero-decay label; byte-identical claim.

How I would proceed

Do not put the best-scoring blend straight into the app. Use the findings to reduce the next experiment to a small, well-defined competition. The practical priority is a model that preserves country differences, handles the measurement process, and survives more than one period.

Choose the forecast question before choosing a winner

Keep 2025 endpoint as the primary result for these registrations. For the next study, declare whether the decision needs a one-year forecast, a seven-year endpoint, or performance across a whole path. Define whether the target is annual life evaluation or its trailing three-year average; add an observation model for the latter. Do not change the target weighting after seeing results.

Carry a small set of challengers forward

Use persistence and #24 country offsets as references. Keep Claude’s decaying offsets as a challenger, alongside the simple 50/50 offset pair. Treat 75/25 offset/decay and 75/25 offset/original as outcome-selected research leads, not champions. If testing them, disclose how they were found. Do not replace #24’s better endpoint GDP path merely because a wellbeing blend wins pooled ladder error.

Test variables by removing them, not by adding a story

Start with matched income-only versus income-plus-health models. Add unemployment, social support, inequality, or other variables only with a declared source, pre-origin availability, missing-data rule, and forecast method for the future input. Test one change at a time on inner folds. For objective wellbeing, keep life expectancy, child survival, income, and schooling as their own outputs; do not invent a ladder conversion.

Protect a genuinely new evaluation

Design and select weights on rolling-origin training folds with adequate history and the same forecast horizon. Use an independent evaluator holding outcomes developers have not accessed, or freeze forecasts now for a future release. Already-seen earlier origins and revised historical panels can be robustness checks, explicitly labelled retrospective. Compare simple averages against any estimated weights with equal selection budgets.

Make subgroup failure a release criterion

Predeclare income groups from origin-year data, crisis-year diagnostics, signed bias, and paired-country uncertainty. Investigate the richest-quarter loss before claiming a broadly dependable default. Include drift and damped-trend baselines in the long objective study; outperforming a constant 1980 level alone is a weak long-run hurdle. Publish losses as well as gains.

Keep policy evidence separate from forecast accuracy

Historical forecast accuracy qualifies a baseline trajectory, not an intervention equation. For policy exploration, require a causal source, explicit units, uncertainty, and a policy-case check for each channel. Show baseline forecast, assumed policy effect, and uncertainty separately. A forecast ensemble may help the baseline without supplying a valid tax, health, AI, or transfer mechanism.
